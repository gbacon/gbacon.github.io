Back in 1995, an acquaintance who worked at EDS pointed me to an interesting website where visitors were presented with a simple form. You could type in a few words, and away it would go, searching the entire web for pages containing those words. These days, search engines are commonplace, but AltaVista represented a cool new idea back then and was an interesting way for DEC to showcase its new Alpha processor.

Although cool, AltaVista was not perfect. I usually found myself sifting through at least three to five pages of results to find the page I wanted. Worse, AltaVista sold keyword placement where advertisers could pay to have their links appear first in the results when users searched for certain words. Those were simpler times, and the idea of being able to query the whole web was still dazzling. Like the typical MS Windows user, I did not know to expect better.

A few years later, I was on the way to Carnegie Mellon University to speak at yapc99, and picked up a copy of Scientific American to pass the time on the plane ride. That particular issue had an article about a new search engine called Google that attempted to solve the relevance problem with a system known as PageRank. According to Google's website, Google interprets a link from page A to page B as a vote by page A for page B. Google looks at more than the sheer volume of votes; it also analyzes the page that casts the vote. Votes cast by pages that are themselves important weigh more heavily and help to make other pages important. I take it for granted now, but it was uncanny how frequently Google's results were right on. I was immediately impressed with the cool technology and the cool millions that employees will likely make when they IPO.

Google continues to maintain that reputation. They recently announced the First Annual Google contest that encourages hackers to submit implementations of interesting new applications of Google's technology. The winner may get to run code against Google's mega-cache and will be the proud recipient of a check for ten grand. Cynics have accused the company of attempting to profit from cheap labor, but this contest could bear delicious fruit. With all the people out there thinking, You know, it would be really cool if, I am anxious to see the winning entry.

In this column, there shall be no unmitigated gushing about the wonders of any technology, no matter how exciting. We must admit that every technology has limitations.

Google has its limitations. In a moment of resignation, a scientist named Jutta Degener wrote about search engines and the audiences they bring to essays.

One the WebCrawler has visited your essay about the naked incomprehension greeting a groups demand for censorship of pornography, the vast majority of its visitors will be searching for porn, naked, and

The question of relevance is therefore not only about finding a page, but also about understanding why the page appears in the results. Search engines can be useful and entertaining, but users should remember that their results reflect the design and assumptions of the search engine. AltaVista showed that searching the web was possible; Google showed how much better ranking could make it.

Those were simpler times, and the ability to query the whole web was still dazzling. A search engine can be a powerful tool, but an essay can attract readers for reasons its author never intended. The technology is exciting precisely because it connects ideas, people, and accidental audiences.
