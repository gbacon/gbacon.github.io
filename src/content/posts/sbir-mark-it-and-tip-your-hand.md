During the Q&amp;A after my [2026 Catalyst workshop talk](/talks/2026-end-in-mind), the host raised something she sees in her own coaching. Some clients are almost afraid to say too much in a proposal. They don’t want to give their idea away, and they worry it will be stolen.

That’s a fantastic question, and the instinct behind it is reasonable. But it can also sink a proposal. Here’s how I think about it.

*Views here are my own, not those of any employer or client. I have a PhD in computer science. I can tell you a lot about Turing machines and the halting problem, but I’m not an attorney. For your specific situation, especially if you’re bringing existing IP into a proposal, get qualified legal advice.*

## First, what you keep

In “[Begin with the End in Mind](/posts/begin-with-the-end-in-mind-sbir-sttr)” I noted that an SBIR award isn’t a loan, doesn’t take equity, and doesn’t take your IP. It’s not *Shark Tank*. You can develop patents, copyrights, and other intellectual property with government R&amp;D money and walk away owning them, whether you market them to partners or use them to attract an acquirer.

The government does protect its investment. If it pours a few million dollars of seed money into your prototype, it doesn’t want to lose the ability to use that technology if your company goes belly-up or simply loses interest. (That happens. Some companies just ghost their customer.) Honestly, if you pay for something to be developed, you should be able to keep using it.

My layman’s summary, worth exactly what you paid for it: government *unlimited* rights mean the government can do almost anything with the data. More limited rights, such as government purpose rights, restrict what it can do or for how long. [UNCLEAR: in the talk I described a common pattern where the small business keeps rights for an initial period, “maybe the first five years,” before the government gets fuller rights. Current SBA policy sets a 20-year SBIR protection period, so check this before publishing.]

Here’s the verifiable rule. According to [SBIR.gov’s data rights tutorial](https://www.sbir.gov/tutorials/data-rights/tutorial-2), SBA’s May&nbsp;2019 policy directive set a uniform 20-year SBIR/STTR data protection period that starts on the date of award. During that period the government gets a limited license to use SBIR data but can’t disclose it. Read the data-rights clauses in your solicitation and contract. Your proposal is also where you make your data rights assertions for anything you’re bringing in that you already own.

## Mark it

The solicitation tells you how to mark proprietary information in a Phase&nbsp;I or Phase&nbsp;II proposal. Follow those instructions exactly. In DoD, the submission form on the [Defense SBIR/STTR Innovation Portal](https://www.dodsbirsttr.mil/) asks which pages of your proposal contain proprietary information.

Once information is marked proprietary, the government has an obligation to protect it and may not hand it to other contractors. That obligation depends on proper marking, though. The [Department of Energy’s summary of Phase&nbsp;III provisions](https://www.energy.gov/documents/phase-iii-awards-statutory-policy-provisions) stresses that protection applies to *properly marked* SBIR/STTR data and gives the legend to use. If you’re worried something could be given away, mark it.

## Then tip your hand

Here’s the part people get wrong. Marking your pages protects you. Saying nothing doesn’t help you at all.

If your technical volume amounts to “our amazing proprietary process will do great things,” you’re asking the evaluator to trust you. You have to give them something to grade. Evaluators can only score what’s written down in black and white, so a proposal that hides everything scores like a proposal with nothing in it.

So tip enough of your hand to show the evaluator it’s real. You don’t have to give away all of the secret sauce. Do give enough that a skeptical reviewer can see how it works and why it should. If the technology is already working somewhere, say what it does. Use your proprietary results to back up your claims.

In the talk I made up an example to show what I mean. Instead of “our process is faster and cleaner,” write something like “we can do this 33% faster and cut emissions by 67%, based on proprietary studies we commissioned with an independent testing organization.” The numbers are illustrative, not real results. The point is to make specific, quantified claims backed by evidence, on pages you’ve marked properly.

## Ideas aren’t the scarce part

There’s an old saying: don’t worry about people stealing your ideas. If they’re any good, you’ll have to shove them down people’s throats. In the talk I told the story of a Huntsville company with an inflatable satellite antenna concept that couldn’t get traction until its founder [UNCLEAR: GATR founder’s name; the captions say “Paul Giro”; confirm spelling before naming him] drove prototypes from Huntsville to New Orleans after Hurricane Katrina and got communications back up. Nobody stole the idea. The hard part was getting anyone to care.

Your SBIR proposal has the same problem. The risk of an evaluator lifting your idea is small, and the law gives you tools to manage it. The risk of an evaluator not believing you is large. Protect what matters, then make your case. For more on making that case, see [A Good SBIR Proposal Isn’t Written, It’s Rewritten](/posts/sbir-proposal-rewritten).
