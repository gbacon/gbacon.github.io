Here’s a scene I warned about in my [2026 Catalyst workshop talk](/talks/2026-end-in-mind). You get through Phase&nbsp;I and then Phase&nbsp;II, with \$1.5 to \$2 million spent. You’ve built your prototype, and the customer is excited. Then what?

Then you wait. If you just sit there, it could be a year or two.

People call this the SBIR valley of death, the gap between the end of Phase&nbsp;II and the start of Phase&nbsp;III. I sometimes call it the bathtub of death. It’s where good prototypes go to die. This post covers how to plan for it. It follows “[Begin with the End in Mind](/posts/begin-with-the-end-in-mind-sbir-sttr),” which explains why Phase&nbsp;III should shape your proposal from the first page.

*Views here are my own, not those of any employer or client.*

## It isn’t a staircase

On paper, SBIR looks like a neat staircase: Phase&nbsp;I, then Phase&nbsp;II, then Phase&nbsp;III. Real paths rarely look like that. The founder of GATR, the Huntsville inflatable-satellite-antenna company I mentioned in the talk, gives a presentation he calls the spaghetti chart [UNCLEAR: exact name of the talk or chart; the captions say “the spaghetti chart, the spaghetti presentation”]. He had built SBIR prototypes and wasn’t getting anywhere. Traction came from getting out and hustling: he drove prototypes to New Orleans after Hurricane Katrina and got communications back up. [UNCLEAR: in the talk I said this led to “multiple hundreds of millions of dollars” of task orders and a sale of the company. Verify against a public source before publishing.]

Most of the time, expect a nonlinear, twisty path, and expect to have to push.

## Phase&nbsp;II money goes faster than you think

A typical Phase&nbsp;II runs 18 months to two years and might be \$1.5 to \$2.5 million. (Check [SBIR.gov](https://www.sbir.gov/about) for current limits.) It’s tempting to think that’s all the money in the world. It isn’t. Once you build a wrap rate with overhead, G&amp;A, fringe, travel, and other direct costs, the money disappears quickly. Don’t plan on having extra left to fund the gap.

## Somebody has to be working Phase&nbsp;III from day one

This is the main point. When the Phase&nbsp;II award comes in, it’s easy to think it changes everything. It doesn’t, unless someone on your team is already working on Phase&nbsp;III commercialization. Start when you win Phase&nbsp;I, and certainly no later than Phase&nbsp;II.

In practice, that means:

* **Name the customer, the prime, and the vehicle.** Which program would use this? Which primes would you team with? Are you already a sub on a contract vehicle that could carry it? Write that in your Phase&nbsp;I proposal, then spend Phase&nbsp;I and Phase&nbsp;II making it true.
* **Line up partners early, and get it in writing.** In the Q&amp;A, someone working on modular organic waste conversion asked how important it is to have pilot-site interest or community partners already in Phase&nbsp;I. My answer: as much as you can get. A letter of support from a potential prime, site, or government customer can go in your Phase&nbsp;I supporting documents and adds weight to your idea. Saying “we already have an agreement in place and have done similar work together successfully” beats “I’m going to go talk to…” every time. It tells the evaluator this isn’t PowerPoint engineering or a visit from the good-idea fairy.
* **Use what you’ve already done.** Earlier awards are proof that you can deliver. Some topics are Direct to Phase&nbsp;II (DP2) and don’t accept Phase&nbsp;I proposals at all. If you’ve already shown feasibility, you may qualify.
* **Know your easy buttons.** A Phase&nbsp;III can be sole-sourced and funded with any color of money outside the SBIR/STTR set-aside. If the government wants to continue the work, it has to justify not giving it to you. See [The Phase&nbsp;III Easy Buttons](/posts/sbir-phase-iii-easy-buttons).

## The side benefits that help you across

Even if Phase&nbsp;III takes longer than you hoped, the journey builds things that help you get across:

* **An approved accounting system.** Phase&nbsp;II awards are typically cost-plus-fixed-fee, while Phase&nbsp;I is usually firm-fixed-price. A Phase&nbsp;II may leave you with an approved accounting system, which opens doors to prime contracts and makes you a more attractive subcontractor.
* **Business development in miniature.** An SBIR takes you through the whole business development cycle, from concept to capture, qualification, pursuit, bid, win, execution, and closeout. That experience carries over.
* **Primes who come knocking.** Large primes notice SBIR awards. A key capability with an SBIR behind it is a reason for them to bring you onto their team.

## Help from the reauthorization

The [2026 reauthorization](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IN12705), which runs through September&nbsp;30, 2031, includes help aimed at this exact gap. It adds Phase&nbsp;III training for contracting officers, enhanced technical and business assistance (TABA), and Strategic Breakthrough Awards of up to \$30M for companies with a prior Phase&nbsp;II and matching funds. That last one could be a game changer for the right company. Check the details against current agency guidance, which is still catching up.

None of that replaces the hustle. Begin with the end in mind, and have someone on your team walking toward Phase&nbsp;III the whole time.
