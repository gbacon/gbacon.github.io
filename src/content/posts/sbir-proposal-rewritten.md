At the end of my [2026 Catalyst workshop talk](/talks/2026-end-in-mind), someone asked whether I won my very first SBIR. I didn’t. I had to learn the ropes the hard way.

I have, however, coached someone who won a Phase&nbsp;I with the very first proposal he ever wrote. I wish I knew what that felt like. What I remember most is how many times we sent him back to the well. Near the end I felt guilty about it. I was sure he’d pour everything in, get a notice of non-selection, and resent us for putting him through the wringer. Then someone at the company texted me that he’d won. I laughed out loud. I was thrilled for him.

The lesson I took from that is something I’ve said for years: **a good proposal isn’t written. A good proposal is rewritten.**

This post builds on “[Begin with the End in Mind](/posts/begin-with-the-end-in-mind-sbir-sttr),” which covers what goes *into* a winning proposal. This one is about the process of getting it there.

*Views here are my own, not those of any employer or client.*

## The romantic version and the real one

There’s a romantic picture of SBIR: a two-person garage company with a cool technical idea writes a cool paper about it, and millions of dollars follow. It doesn’t work that way. You don’t sit down and write the proposal in one pass. You write it, someone who has been down this road tears into it, and you write it again.

## When an experienced reviewer says “you should think about this”

Here’s the hard part, and it’s mostly emotional. You’ve worked hard to get a draft done. The last thing you want is to crack it back open and fight Word over page counts, borders, and margins again. So when someone who has won SBIR or STTR awards before says, “You really ought to think about doing this,” your first reaction may be to resist.

Take the advice to heart anyway. Those suggestions come from hard-won battle scars. Nobody enjoys redoing work, but the rewrite is where the proposal improves.

The reviewer’s side isn’t comfortable either. When I’m the one saying “No, that’s not quite it, go back,” I start to feel like I’m nitpicking. That’s fine. The discomfort on both sides is a sign the process is working.

If you don’t have an experienced reviewer in-house, find one. Local entrepreneurship centers, SBIR assistance programs, people who’ve been there before, and paid proposal-support firms can all help. Paid help costs money, but if your company can afford it, it will only help.

## Spell it out, then go a little further

Evaluators can only evaluate what you write down in black and white. They can’t make inferences, and it isn’t fair to say afterward, “They should have known that from what I said.” As [DoD’s SBIR/STTR guide](https://www.defensesbirsttr.mil/SBIR-STTR/Guide/) puts it, reviewers judge only what’s in the proposal.

Evaluators also aren’t always technical people, so get out of the tech-speak. My rule of thumb: if you don’t feel a little embarrassed, as though you might be insulting their intelligence, you probably haven’t spelled it out enough. Go a bit past that comfort point. Hold their hand and tell them the story.

Use every bit of space. If a point matters, write it down, bold it, underline it, or pull it out as a pull quote. Figure captions are valuable real estate where you can repeat and reinforce the points you’ve made.

Show, don’t just tell. Your English teacher was right. “Trust me, it’s a cool idea” doesn’t score. Walk the evaluator through what your Phase&nbsp;I will prove, what your Phase&nbsp;II prototype will deliver, and what changes for the government or the warfighter once it’s in their hands. Talk about benefits, not just features.

## The comic book approach

Here’s a test I like. Open your proposal to any page. Does it look interesting? Are there figures, bullet points, section headings, and some color? Or does it open onto fifteen pages of wall-to-wall text that make the evaluator think, “Ugh, shoot me now”?

We call this the comic book approach. Every page should invite the reader in. Maybe yours is the best idea in the world, but if reading it feels like drudgery, the evaluator will skim it to get it off their plate. The proposal is a sales document, so make it look appealing.

## Don’t spend your pages on the fun part

The rewrite is also where you fix the balance. Technical people tend to go on and on about the technology because it’s fascinating to them. Then they wave their hands at the work plan (“at the end of six months, a miracle occurs”) because they hate fighting with scheduling tools. Then comes a one-paragraph commercialization plan that amounts to “I’ll look up primes in Huntsville on Google and call them.” That won’t get it done.

Hook the reader with the technology, then use your limited page count to cover everything else: the Phase&nbsp;I outcome, the Phase&nbsp;II prototype, and how you’ll start marketing in Phase&nbsp;I to connect with Phase&nbsp;II and Phase&nbsp;III partners. If the solicitation suggests a one-page commercialization plan, I’d write at least two, as long as the page limits allow it. Otherwise you’ll join the many first-time submitters whose proposals were high-level and uncompelling.

## Plan the time to rewrite

All of this takes time. Read the solicitation early, including the component instructions, and stitch them into one end-to-end list of requirements. Then plan a schedule that leaves room for more than one full draft and more than one round of review. If your plan only has time for writing, you haven’t planned for winning.
