When I talk to people who are new to SBIR and STTR, they usually see the dollar figures first. A Phase&nbsp;I is a couple of hundred thousand dollars, and a Phase&nbsp;II is a couple of million. Those numbers matter, but they aren’t what makes the program special. What makes it special is what an award lets you do *after* the SBIR money runs out.

In “[Begin with the End in Mind](/posts/begin-with-the-end-in-mind-sbir-sttr)” I argued that you should plan your Phase&nbsp;III before you write your Phase&nbsp;I. This post is about why Phase&nbsp;III is such a good place to end up. I call these the easy buttons, and I covered them in my [2026 Catalyst workshop talk](/talks/2026-end-in-mind).

*Views here are my own, not those of any employer or client. I’m not an attorney, and none of this is legal advice.*

## Phase&nbsp;III is a bit of a misnomer

Phase&nbsp;I is feasibility and Phase&nbsp;II is a prototype. Both are paid for out of the SBIR/STTR set-aside. Phase&nbsp;III is different. There’s no SBIR set-aside money in it at all. It’s the point where your product stands on its own two feet and funds its own development and maintenance, whether you sell to the government, to commercial customers, or to both.

So why call it a phase? Because the work still carries SBIR status, and that status brings rights with it.

## Easy button 1: sole-source, collaborative awards

In a normal competitive procurement, the government and the bidders have to stay on opposite sides of a wall. The government writes requirements, you guess what they really meant, and you propose something they kind of like. Too often, at the end of the period of performance, the customer says, “That isn’t what we wanted.” The contractor built exactly what the requirement said, and everyone is unhappy.

A Phase&nbsp;III direct award can avoid that. Because the work extends something the government already competed through Phase&nbsp;I or Phase&nbsp;II, the government and the small business can sit down together and write the statement of work collaboratively. The question becomes “Let’s figure out exactly what you want,” not “Guess what we want.”

The law backs this up. Under [15 U.S.C. §&nbsp;638(r)(4)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/638), agencies are to issue Phase&nbsp;III awards, including sole-source awards, to the SBIR and STTR awardees that developed the technology “to the greatest extent practicable.” [SBIR.gov’s data rights tutorial](https://www.sbir.gov/tutorials/data-rights/tutorial-4) calls this the “Phase&nbsp;III mandate.”

## Easy button 2: the tables turn

This is the part people are most surprised to hear. If the government wants to continue developing technology that came out of your Phase&nbsp;I or Phase&nbsp;II, the burden flips. They have to justify why the work should *not* go to the small business that developed it.

There are limits. The Department of Energy’s [summary of Phase&nbsp;III provisions](https://www.energy.gov/documents/phase-iii-awards-statutory-policy-provisions) notes that the preference applies when an agency is seeking the specific technology you developed. It doesn’t force an agency to hand you work it is competing openly for some other solution. Still, when it applies, it’s a strong position for a small company.

## Easy button 3: flexibility

As I understand the rules, a Phase&nbsp;III has a lot of room to move:

* There’s no limit on how many Phase&nbsp;IIIs a single Phase&nbsp;I or Phase&nbsp;II can lead to.
* Phase&nbsp;III can be funded with any color of money outside the SBIR/STTR set-aside.
* It can be a prime contract or a subcontract.
* It can be a FAR-based contract or an Other Transaction Agreement.

I’ve seen that last point firsthand. The Army SBIR I mentioned in the [anchor post](/posts/begin-with-the-end-in-mind-sbir-sttr) moved into Phase&nbsp;III through an Other Transaction Agreement.

## Even a humble Phase&nbsp;I is a door

People underrate this one. Even a six-month Phase&nbsp;I for \$150,000 is a competed contract with Uncle Sam. It can be modified, and it can become a pivot point that most people never think about.

It also changes how large primes see you. If you don’t hold a socioeconomic certification, you may have run into primes that only want to talk to you if you do. An SBIR award is a different kind of differentiator. When a prime asks, “Why do we need you?”, you can say, “I’ve got this technology, and I’ve got an SBIR Phase&nbsp;I and Phase&nbsp;II on it.” I know several small businesses in Huntsville that had large, well-known primes come knocking because of exactly that.

## Data rights ride along

Phase&nbsp;III status also brings SBIR data rights with it. Since SBA’s May&nbsp;2019 policy directive, [the protection period is 20&nbsp;years from the date of award](https://www.sbir.gov/tutorials/data-rights/tutorial-2), and that covers properly marked data generated during Phase&nbsp;III work. The important words there are “properly marked.” I’ll say more about marking in a [later post on protecting your ideas](/posts/sbir-mark-it-and-tip-your-hand).

## What this means for your Phase&nbsp;I

None of this happens automatically. A Phase&nbsp;III preference only helps if a customer actually wants what you built. That’s why I keep saying to begin with the end in mind. Name the customer, the program, and the vehicle in your Phase&nbsp;I proposal, then use Phase&nbsp;II to make that customer want to use the easy button. How to get through the gap between Phase&nbsp;II and that first Phase&nbsp;III is the subject of [Crossing the SBIR Valley of Death](/posts/sbir-valley-of-death).
